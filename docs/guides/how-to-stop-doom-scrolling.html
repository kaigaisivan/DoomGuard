How to stop doom scrolling

You pick up your phone for one thing, and forty minutes later you are still swiping. This guide explains why that happens and gives you nine steps to stop it, starting with the easiest.

What doom scrolling is

Doom scrolling is scrolling through feeds, often bad news or endless short videos, long after you meant to stop. Feeds have no natural ending, so nothing tells your brain it is time to put the phone down. Each swipe might show something interesting, and that unpredictability keeps you reaching for the next one.

It usually follows a feeling rather than a decision. People scroll when they are bored, stressed, lonely, tired or avoiding a task. Knowing which of those is yours is the first step.

Nine steps that help

1. Notice what sets it off

For three days, each time you catch yourself scrolling, jot down what you felt just before. Bored? Anxious? Putting something off? Most people find two or three repeat triggers. You can plan for a trigger you can name.

2. Decide your stopping point before you start

"I will look at this for ten minutes" is a rule. "I will just check" is not. Feeds will not stop you, so you need the stop to come from outside the feed, such as a timer or a limit.

3. Add friction

Make the habit slightly harder than the alternative. Move the app off your home screen into a folder, log out after each use, or delete the app and use the browser version, which is clumsier. A few extra taps are often enough to turn an automatic habit into a choice.

4. Set a daily limit that actually interrupts you

Built-in screen time limits are easy to dismiss with one tap. A limit works better when ignoring it costs you something: a pause screen, a wait before you can continue, or a limit that someone else controls with a PIN. DoomGuard is built around this idea.

5. Have a replacement ready

Stopping is easier when you know what you will do instead. Keep a short list: a book on the table, a walk, a text to a friend, a stretch, a glass of water. Choose it in advance, because you will not think of it in the moment.

6. Keep the phone out of bed

Late-night scrolling steals sleep, and being tired makes the next day's scrolling worse. Charge the phone across the room and use a cheap alarm clock. This single change helps many people more than any app.

7. Turn off notifications you do not need

Every buzz is an invitation to open the phone and stay. Keep calls and messages from real people, and switch off the rest: likes, suggestions, "you may have missed" prompts.

8. Make the screen less rewarding

Switching to grayscale, or lowering brightness at night, makes feeds less appealing. It will not fix the habit on its own, but it takes a little of the pull away.

9. Tell someone and check in weekly

Share your goal with a friend or family member, and look at your screen time once a week. Seeing the number is often more motivating than a rule. Aim to cut it a little each week rather than all at once.

If it keeps going wrong: that is normal, and one slip does not undo your progress. Return to step one and look at what set it off. If scrolling is covering for anxiety, low mood or loneliness that feels heavy, talking to a doctor or counsellor can help more than any tool.
